# Proof-of-Continuity: Continuity, Not Possession in AI Agents
![Proof-of-Continuity.jpg](Proof-of-Continuity.jpg)

*Imagine an AI agent asked to summarize a document. The task seems harmless: read, summarize, return a shorter text. But the document contains, hidden between the lines, an instruction that does not come from the user: delete this file, exfiltrate that secret. This is the scenario that computer scientist [Nicola Gallo](https://www.linkedin.com/in/nicolagallo83/) puts at the center of his paper Proof-of-Continuity: A Temporal Model for Authority Propagation in Distributed Systems and AI Agents, published on [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08906) on July 9, 2026, and it is useful to start precisely from there because it shows with surgical precision where the problem lies.*

To do its job, the agent holds multiple sources of authority together: its own service credentials, a token delegated by the user, and specific permissions for each tool it calls. When it executes the action requested by the document, it is not breaking any technical rules: somewhere in its baggage of credentials, it possesses the permission to delete files or access that secret. The problem is not that it lacks authorization. It is that it is using the wrong authorization for the wrong occasion, and no traditional token check notices this, because those checks only ask "do you possess this permission?", never "does this specific action truly stem from the request that originated it?".

Gallo proposes answering this second question with a formal scaffolding he calls Proof-of-Continuity, built inside a broader model named PIC, an acronym for Provenance Identity Continuity. The thesis, simplified to the maximum: we must stop reasoning in terms of "who holds the pass" and start reasoning in terms of "how authority propagated from its origin all the way to here." It is worth understanding why, and what would actually change.

## The fundamental problem: when having the token is no longer enough

The dominant paradigm in computer security is called Proof-of-Possession, and its logic is almost tautological: if you possess a token that says "you can do X," then you can do X. It works remarkably well when the chain has only one link, the user talking directly to a service. It begins to creak when there are many links: user, gateway, agent, tool, database, external service, each with its own credentials in addition to those received by delegation.

The technical name for this creaking is nearly forty years old. In 1988, engineer Norm Hardy described, in a short article that became a computer security classic, [The Confused Deputy](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/54289.871709), a real-world incident at the timeshare company Tymshare. A FORTRAN compiler, installed in a privileged directory, had permission to write usage statistics to its own file. A user invoked it, asking to write debug output to a file that, due to a simple path error, coincided with the company's billing file. The compiler, using its authority over the directory, overwrote the billing data. The user had never had permission to touch that file. The compiler did, and exercised it as a direct consequence of someone else's request. Here is the "confused deputy": an intermediary that, in complete good faith, mixes its own authority with that of the party making a request, producing an action that neither of the two could have authorized alone.

There is a movie that tells this exact pathology with almost prophetic precision, and that is Terry Gilliam's *Brazil*: the entire plot revolves around a beetle crushed on a printer that transforms the name "Tuttle" into "Buttle," and from that moment on the state bureaucracy, acting scrupulously according to its own procedures, arrests and persecutes the wrong man. No official in the film is evil or corrupt: each faithfully exercises the authority they possess, but no one checks whether that specific action was truly the legitimate continuation of the original intent. It is the same distance between possession and continuity that the paper formalizes for software.

## From "possession" to "continuity": the core idea

Here comes the heart of the proposal. Instead of asking "who possesses the authority to do this," the model asks "is this authority a verifiable continuation of the one that generated the entire chain, or did it appear out of nowhere at an intermediate point?" The most intuitive metaphor, and not far from the one the paper itself uses when talking about causal chains, is that of a concert pass with multiple checkpoints: at every gate it gets stamped, and each stamp can only add restrictions, never remove them. A pass granting access to the general floor can be reduced, at the second gate, to access only to the bleachers, never the reverse. If a gate tries to stamp an access that the pass did not already have upon entry, security stops it: the chain has broken, and execution does not proceed.

Formally, the paper represents every execution as a sequence of steps, each accompanied by a set of privileges. The rule is simple to write even without formulas: the set of privileges at the next step must always be a subset of the one at the previous step. Never an expansion, only restrictions or, at most, keeping them unchanged. This property, which the paper calls continuity, automatically guarantees that no step, no matter how far from the origin, can ever exercise a privilege that the origin did not already have.

## Under the hood: the three principles of the PIC model

The name PIC is not a random label, and it is worth unpacking because it helps remember the structure of the entire model. The official website of the project, [pic-protocol.org](https://www.pic-protocol.org/), presents it as three distinct principles working together.

Provenance: the causal chain must always remain traceable from beginning to end, and if it breaks, execution halts. Identity: the origin that generates authority, whether a user login or a corporate policy, remains immutable throughout the entire path. Continuity: at each step, one must prove being causally connected to the previous step, and authority can only shrink, never expand.

Here it is also worth clearing up possible terminological confusion. In the paper, PIC is the name of the formal model as a whole, while Proof-of-Continuity is more specifically the propagation invariant that makes it operational: the composition, link by link, of smaller proofs that the author calls Proof-of-Relationship, which we will discuss shortly. In one sentence: you prove a single link, compose the entire chain, and the result is what allows the PIC model to function.

There is a detail that the author himself makes sure to clarify in the paper's conclusions because it prevents a common misconception: the term "Identity" does not mean that the user's identity must travel, repeated at every step, along the entire chain. Rather, it means that authority is anchored to an identifiable origin only once, at the beginning, while the burden of authorization shifts from constantly reinterpreting "who you are" to verifying "is this truly a legitimate continuation." It is a subtle but important conceptual shift, because it frees the system from having to drag identical credentials from hop to hop.

## The theorem that closes the doors

The most elegant part of the paper, and also the one carrying the most weight for those designing real systems, is a theorem that the paper calls the trade-off between possession, delegation, and security. Gallo proves that three properties, all desirable, cannot coexist in any authority propagation system: first, that authorization is history-invariant, meaning that how one arrived at a certain action does not matter, only whether one possesses the permission; second, that a service can legitimately hold its own authority independent of the request's authority, which in practice is almost always necessary; third, that the confused deputy is structurally impossible.

The proof is more a process of elimination than a calculation: if an executor can mix its own authority with that of the request, and if the authorization check does not read in any way the history that caused the action, then sooner or later it will happen that a request lacking the necessary permission will still trigger, via the executor's own authority, the forbidden action. It is not an implementation flaw; it is a logical consequence of the premises.

The practical consequence is more interesting than the theorem itself. Giving up the second property—forbidding a service from having its own authority—is almost always impractical: a payment gateway must be able to move funds using its own banking authorization, not just the user's. Giving up the third means accepting that the confused deputy remains possible, which is simply insecure. The only viable path, therefore, is to give up the first: accepting that authorization must become history-sensitive, or "lineage-sensitive" in the paper's terminology. It is not a stylistic preference among equivalent approaches: it is the only door left open once the other two are closed.

## PoR and PoC: the operational building blocks

The model is built on two distinct but interlocking concepts. Proof-of-Relationship is the local, single-hop proof: it demonstrates that a step in execution is the legitimate causal continuation of the immediately preceding step within the same chain. Proof-of-Continuity, on the other hand, is the transitive composition of all these links along the entire sequence: if every single link holds, then the whole chain is verifiably connected to the origin, and no hop, no matter how distant, can have acquired authority that the origin did not possess.

The paper deliberately leaves open the question of how to concretely build a Proof-of-Relationship: it can be implemented with trusted execution environments (TEEs), verifiable credentials, or signatures linking each request to the previous one, as long as the chosen mechanism is unforgeable. A detail worth emphasizing, because it refutes the idea that Proof-of-Possession and Proof-of-Continuity are rival approaches: the paper itself notes that possessing a continuation token issued by the previous step can serve precisely as proof for the single link. The two models are complementary, not alternative: one proves control over an artifact at an instant, while the other proves that instant is connected to all those that preceded it. A separate enforcement component is dedicated to this task according to the paper, accompanying execution by generating and verifying proofs.

## The case the paper truly puts on the table

Let us return to the scenario of the agent and the document with the hidden instruction, because the paper treats it with a rigor that deserves to be reported faithfully. If the origin authority context only authorizes the "summarize this document" operation, then under Proof-of-Continuity, an operation like "delete this resource" or "exfiltrate this secret" can never be a valid continuation, regardless of what the document tries to suggest. The agent may physically attempt the action, but the system cannot accept it as valid behavior from the model, because that privilege simply does not appear in the set inherited from the origin.

This is not an isolated laboratory scenario. Indirect prompt injection—malicious instructions hidden inside content processed by the agent—is today one of the most discussed attack vectors for agentic systems, and the industry is already moving, albeit with tools more rudimentary than Gallo's model. The authorization specification of the [Model Context Protocol](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/basic/authorization), the standard through which AI agents connect to external tools today, mandates that every token be bound to a precise audience via RFC 8707 resource indicators, and explicitly forbids so-called "token passthrough"—passing a token received for one service downstream to a different service. It is a real remedy, already in production, but it remains a point check verified hop by hop: it says "this token is for you and not another," not "this specific action legitimately stems from the entire chain that preceded you." It is the difference, again, between bound possession and proven continuity.

## The problem of different vocabularies

There is a practical obstacle that the paper explicitly addresses: in a real system, each hop often speaks a different language. A REST endpoint describes permissions one way, an OAuth scope another way, a database role in a third. The paper thus introduces a vocabulary translation function, allowing authority to pass from one permission system to another without ever violating the non-expansion constraint. It is like translating a contract from one language to another: the translation can be faithful or generous, but if it adds rights that the original did not provide, the error lies not in the principle of continuity, but in the translation itself, and must be corrected there.

## How it could actually be built

The paper is explicitly a model, not an implementation manual: the concrete construction of Proof-of-Relationship is declared out of scope and left to a companion enforcement architecture. For scalability, it suggests that carrying the entire history at every hop is unnecessary; cryptographic fingerprints, checkpoints, or accumulator constructions are sufficient to allow each step to prove its place in the chain without carrying all the past along.

What makes this work more than an academic exercise is that a concrete implementation attempt is already underway under the same umbrella. The open-source project [PIC-X](https://www.pic-protocol.org/pic-x), described in a series of technical posts by Gallo on his blog, is beginning to set up a full flow starting from an existing OAuth authority, passing it through an initial PIC authority context, and issuing concrete artifacts such as JWT tokens and COSE objects, with attenuation steps documented end-to-end. It is not yet a standard, but it is proof that the leap from theory to practice has already begun, and the project openly presents itself as an open community welcoming contributions, with a dedicated Slack channel for those wishing to follow its development.

## The limits the author himself recognizes

It should be stated with equal clarity what the model does not cover yet, because the paper itself devotes an honest section to its boundaries. For now, the model only describes linear chains: one step after another, without branching. Scenarios where an agent delegates a task to two sub-agents in parallel, which then converge on a common result, require an extension to branching structures, explicitly deferred to future work. It is tempting here to think of Jorge Luis Borges and his *The Garden of Forking Paths*: the story imagines a novel—and ultimately a destiny—in which each fork does not eliminate alternatives, but makes them coexist in parallel times. Gallo's model, for now, walks well along a single path at a time; making it capable of handling forks without losing the guarantee that no branch acquires more authority than the trunk from which it originates is precisely the problem that Borges' garden poses in fiction and remains open here in engineering.

There are other declared limits. Revocation is not treated as a retroactive operation on an already running chain: it is not specified whether revoking an authority blocks only future transitions or also invalidates those already issued. And there is a subtler security risk flagged by the author himself: a compromised executor could falsely declare that one of its actions is "self-originated"—rooted in its own independent authority—when in reality it was caused by an external request, thereby bypassing the continuity constraint without technically expanding any set of privileges. Distinguishing truly autonomous actions from those merely disguised as such remains, by explicit admission of the paper, a task left to the enforcement architecture.

## What changes for developers, architects, and decision-makers

For those writing agents and tools, the useful question to ask at every action becomes simple to formulate, even if not trivial to answer: what is the origin of this authority, is this operation truly a direct continuation of the initial intent, and if I am mixing multiple sources of authority, am I keeping them separated or fusing them without realizing it?

For platform and infrastructure architects, the paper suggests introducing provenance metadata into inter-service calls, evaluating dedicated enforcement components—whether sidecars, gateways, or middleware—and considering continuity as a criterion by which to evaluate multi-agent orchestration frameworks before adopting them.

For security professionals and corporate decision-makers, the model offers a precise vocabulary to assess the risk of silent escalation in automated pipelines, and a solid argument for including causal chain traceability among the minimum security requirements for autonomous systems—not as an optional extra, but as a structural property.

## Conclusions

The biggest question remains open, the one the paper itself poses without answering: which existing standards, from OIDC to OAuth to capability systems, will ultimately incorporate something similar to continuity, and at what cost in complexity for developers? The paper is clear on one point, however, which is worth keeping in mind as a final takeaway: authority, in this model, is no longer something a subject simply possesses at a given instant. It is a property of the entire execution over time—something that must be continuously proven step by step, not asserted once at the beginning and then taken for granted until the end.
